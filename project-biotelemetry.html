Overview

I designed a coin-cell-powered wireless bio-sensing platform for non-invasive aquatic biometric monitoring. With electrodes submerged in the tank water, the system is designed to measure the fish's cardiac signal through water conductivity alone, with no implants or restraints.

Originally conceived as a plant action-potential recorder, the architecture was pivoted via hardware-software co-design so the same board can serve as an aquatic monitor, with the species-specific changes made in firmware. The analog front-end stays wide; all species-specific signal extraction is handled in firmware.

What I Built

  • Custom 4-layer mixed-signal PCB — KiCad schematic, full layout with separated analog/digital domains, RF keepout zone, and Gerbers prepared for JLCPCB SMT assembly; I hand-assembled and bench-tested the prototype
  • Wideband analog front-end — AD8232 biopotential amplifier with a sub-0.5 Hz HPF (DC block) and a Sallen-Key LPF measured at −3 dB = 42.5 Hz, avoiding rigid brick-wall filters to allow software-defined signal extraction
  • Active noise cancellation — Right Leg Drive electrode submerged in tank water is designed to inject an inverted common-mode signal, intended to cancel 60 Hz EMI at the source
  • Zephyr firmware on the Cortex-M4F — 60 Hz notch, per-species QRS band-pass and a Pan-Tompkins-style beat detector; band, thresholds and species list are editable over BLE and saved to flash
  • Host tools and tests — Python GUI for species setup, calibration and baselines, plus a test suite that runs the real C detector and config parser against simulated recordings
  • RF layout — strict copper keepout beneath the MDBT50Q-1MV2's ceramic antenna across all four layers to maximize Bluetooth range

Problem

  • Fish ECG is a few hundred µV, and tank water adds 60 Hz mains hum plus slow electrode drift that can saturate an amplifier.
  • I wanted a sensor that never touches the animal: electrodes sit in the water, so no implants or restraint.

Lessons

  • A Sallen-Key with equal capacitors has Q = 0.5 and a −3 dB point near 26 Hz. The board uses 56 nF and 27 nF (Q ≈ 0.72), so the −3 dB point sits at 41.6 Hz in theory and 42.5 Hz measured. Quote the −3 dB point, not just f0.
  • Check the programming header pitch before ordering cables. My J1 footprint did not fit my ST-Link leads, so I soldered jumper wires straight to the pins.
  • Power a reworked board through a current limit first. A 2.6 Ω short only showed up as a supply OCP trip and a 0.08 V rail.
  • Keep the analog front-end wide and move species-specific filtering into firmware so one board serves several species.
  • A 1 kHz Zephyr tick on the nRF's 32.768 kHz RTC is really 32 cycles (0.977 ms), so my "250 Hz" sampler ran at 256 Hz and the notch only removed 1.8 dB of 60 Hz. I now sample at 256 Hz on a 32768 Hz tick, which divides exactly.
  • I removed ventilation rate from the output. It was calculated from heart rate, not measured, so reporting it would have been misleading.

The Engineering Challenge

Lab aquatic environments are electrically hostile. 60 Hz mains hum couples strongly into tank water, electrode–water interfaces generate slow DC polarization offsets that saturate amplifiers, and different fish species produce biometric signals across a wide, variable frequency range.

A conventional approach — rigid analog brick-wall filters tuned to a single species — fails on all three counts: it can't adapt to different species without a hardware re-spin, it doesn't handle variable lab noise floors, and tight filter poles near the signal band risk introducing phase distortion into the very signals you're trying to measure.

Design decision: Use a deliberately wide analog passband to capture all biological signals while enforcing two hard limits against ADC saturation. Delegate all species-specific and noise-specific filtering to software DSP running on the nRF52840's hardware FPU.

Wideband Analog Front-End

The analog band runs from a sub-0.5 Hz high-pass to a low-pass with its −3 dB point at 42.5 Hz (measured; 41.6 Hz from the part values) — wide enough to cover every known aquatic vertebrate biometric in the biological action-potential range, tight enough to block the two dominant noise sources before the ADC ever sees them.

Active Shielding — Right Leg Drive

The third electrode is not a passive ground reference. The AD8232's Right Leg Drive (RLD) output actively injects a signal that is the inverted, amplified sum of the common-mode noise seen at both differential inputs. Submerging this electrode in the tank water is expected to create a feedback loop that reduces 60 Hz EMI at the source — a fundamentally superior strategy to filtering it downstream, especially in an unshielded open-water environment.

Digital Signal Processing

The wideband strategy intentionally passes some residual noise to the ADC. The firmware running on the nRF52840's ARM Cortex-M4F is responsible for all signal extraction.

ARM Cortex-M4F FPU Filtering

The nRF52840's hardware FPU runs CMSIS-DSP IIR biquad cascades on every sample. Two filters run before the beat detector:

  • 60 Hz notch — Q = 30 (about 2 Hz wide), much sharper than is practical in analog hardware; regenerated for 50 Hz mains with one script flag
  • Per-species QRS band-pass — 2nd-order Butterworth, 5–25 Hz by default; each species slot stores its own coefficients, which the GUI designs and the firmware checks for stability before using

Hardware-software co-design pivot: This firmware DSP architecture is designed to allow the platform — originally designed for plant electrophysiology — to be repurposed for aquatic bio-telemetry without any hardware modification.

Firmware

The firmware is written for Zephyr (nRF Connect SDK) on the nRF52840. It has not run on the custom board yet; the detector and configuration code have been tested on my PC against simulated recordings (see Testing below).

  • Sampling — SAADC at 256 Hz, 12-bit, 4× oversampled; every sample carries a tick count, so dropped samples are detected and gap-filled
  • Filtering — 60 Hz notch, then the species QRS band-pass; delay lines are primed to the DC level so the 1.5 V AD8232 offset doesn't ring through on start
  • Detection — 20 ms RMS envelope, adaptive signal/noise thresholds, refractory period and an R-R outlier gate; oversized transients are counted as artifacts (cough or movement) and never as beats
  • Telemetry — a 26-byte packet per detected beat (BPM, HRV RMSSD, artifacts/min, beat amplitude, status), plus a 1 Hz NO_SIGNAL packet when no beat is seen for 5 s
  • Health calls only after a baseline — no literature heart-rate limits are compiled in; a species reports UNCALIBRATED until I record that fish at rest and apply measured limits
  • Persistence — species list, bands, limits and detector thresholds are saved to NVS flash, validated again at boot, and ignored if they were made for a different sample rate
Firmware v3.4 signal path: 256 Hz sampling, notch, species band-pass, beat detector, metrics, BLE and flash.
Firmware Architecture — Zephyr RTOS

The firmware (v3.4.0) is split into four preemptive Zephyr threads connected by message queues, so sampling timing never depends on DSP or BLE work.

ThreadStackPriorityJob
adc_thread1024 B2Wakes on a semaphore from a 256 Hz k_timer, reads the SAADC, posts the sample and its tick count
dsp_thread3072 B3Notch + band-pass, QRS detection, BPM / HRV / artifact rate, status, posts to telem_queue
ble_thread2048 B5Sends the 26-byte telemetry packet as a GATT notification
aux_thread1536 B6Sends configuration replies and the raw-ADC calibration stream
  • Timing — the sample clock is a k_timer of exactly 128 RTC ticks whose expiry gives a semaphore, so the ISR stays short; all beat timing comes from the sample index, not wall-clock time
  • Queues — k_msgq between stages: 32 raw samples (125 ms of slack), 4 telemetry packets, 16 aux packets; puts use K_NO_WAIT so a slow stage drops data instead of blocking the sampler
  • Shared filter state — a k_mutex guards the active species profile and bandpass coefficients, so a species change over BLE can re-initialize the filter safely while the DSP thread runs
  • DSP — CMSIS-DSP float biquad cascades on the Cortex-M4F: one notch stage and a two-stage band-pass
  • Species slots — eight slots, four pre-named (glass catfish, Atlantic silverside, inland silverside, Chromis viridis); slots can be added, renamed or deleted over BLE, and each has its own band and measured limits
  • GATT service — species select, telemetry notify, acquisition start/stop, a command/response configuration characteristic, and a raw-ADC stream for calibration

Before the board is up, I test the firmware's detector (qrs_detect.c) and configuration parser (cfg_proto.c) on my PC. PCB Layout & Manufacturing

4-Layer Stackup & RF Keepout

The 4-layer stackup is designed with dedicated power and ground planes to support domain separation between the noise-sensitive analog measurement path and the switching digital/RF domain. A strict copper keepout zone is enforced on all four layers directly beneath the MDBT50Q-1MV2's integrated 2.4 GHz ceramic antenna. Removing ground copper from the antenna footprint is intended to keep the ground plane from loading the antenna, to maximize expected Bluetooth range in the lab.

Analog vs digital/RF domains, 4-layer stack (signals / ground / power / signals), antenna keepout, C3 flow-through decoupling at the AD8232 supply.

Signal Integrity — Flow-Through Decoupling

High-frequency switching transients from the nRF52840's internal DC-DC converter are intended to be blocked from coupling into the analog measurement path via flow-through power decoupling on the AD8232 supply pin (C3, 100 nF placed as close to the IC pin as the layout permits). The goal is to keep digital switching noise from appearing as correlated interference in the biopotential measurement.

DFM — Via-in-Pad Mitigation & Automated Assembly

Several SMD passive footprints in the design placed vias within the pad copper. Without mitigation, solder wicks through the via during reflow, causing insufficient joints and potential opens. Each instance was identified in layout review and resolved by offsetting or tenting the via before Gerber generation.

The BOM, CPL, and POS files were optimized for JLCPCB's automated SMT pick-and-place service, with all standard passives and commodity ICs assigned to the JLCPCB parts library. The AD8232 and MDBT50Q-1MV2 — both with non-standard footprints — are flagged for manual lab soldering after automated reflow, minimizing per-unit cost while maintaining assembly quality on the critical components.

Bring-up Problems

Wrong-pitch programming header

The J1 SWD header on my board did not match the leads I had for my ST-Link, so nothing would seat. I soldered jumper wires directly to VDD, GND, SWDIO and SWCLK and taped them down. The first loose connections dropped the SWD link mid-session; soldered wires fixed it, and OpenOCD then identified the nRF52840 and flashed it reliably.

The ST-Link lead crimp next to the J1 header it was meant to fit.
The fix: wires soldered to J1 during bench testing.
The supply tripping OCP: the first sign of the VDD–GND short.

Burned chip and a 2.6 Ω short

Midway through testing, the bench supply hit its current limit as soon as I connected it, and a 100 Ω series resistor showed why: 29 mA flowed and the board rail sat at 0.08 V. With power removed, VDD to GND read 2.6 Ω (lead resistance about 0.3 Ω), drifting to 3.4 Ω as it warmed. That is too high for a solder bridge, so I treated it as a damaged part. J1 measured clean, the short was there with SW1 on or off, and no capacitor got warm under a low-voltage current test. I replaced the chip, the short cleared, and I re-flashed and re-ran gain spot checks before trusting the rest of the data.
