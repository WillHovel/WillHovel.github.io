What Drives Me

I am drawn to systems that are hard to measure. Whether it is a fish swimming in a flow tank, a hand making a gesture, or a robot reacting to a track, the interesting engineering problems all share the same structure: you have a physical system generating a continuous, noisy signal, and you want to pull something meaningful and repeatable out of it.

My background in electrical engineering has given me the mathematical foundation to approach these problems rigorously. Coursework in signal processing (ECE 45) and statistics (ECE 109) taught me to think carefully about noise, sampling, and what it actually means for an algorithm to generalize. That foundation shapes every project I work on. I do not just want something that works in demo conditions. I want to understand why it works, and what would make it fail.

The projects on this site reflect that. The fish kinematics pipeline started as a data cleaning problem and turned into a full signal processing system. The sensorized glove required designing a PCB, routing USB differential pairs, reading noisy analog signals, and then figuring out a classification approach that was actually reliable. The line-following robot was an exercise in the feedback loop between algorithm and physical system: even a well-tuned PID controller fails if the mechanical platform behaves inconsistently.

What connects these projects is iteration. None of them worked on the first try. Each required diagnosing what was actually failing and fixing it at the right level of abstraction. That process of building, debugging, and redesigning is where I have learned the most, and it is what I want to keep doing.

Long-term, I want to apply engineering to problems that span fields: bio-inspired robotics, environmental sensing, and tools that make complex data usable by researchers who aren't engineers. I plan to pursue graduate study and eventually run my own research.
