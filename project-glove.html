Sensorized Gesture Glove

I built a glove for logging field observations without looking down or touching a screen. Five flex sensors feed an ESP32-S3 on a board I designed, a small neural network classifies each hand pose as one of my own gestures, and the result shows on an OLED and streams to my computer over USB or BLE. I designed the PCB, soldered and debugged it, wrote the firmware, built the GUI that trains custom classifiers, and measured how well it works.

Problem

Log a category with one hand, eyes up

In field work, looking down to type a note or tap a screen means missing the event, and hands are often wet, muddy or gloved. A glove that classifies a few hand poses lets me log a category with one hand while I keep watching. Each reading is a discrete label, not a stream of analog values, so I put the whole chain on one small board: sensors in, gesture out.

I also wanted to choose the gestures myself, so I built a GUI that trains a classifier for whatever gestures I define.

Design

What I built

  • Board: custom KiCad PCB with an ESP32-S3-Mini-1, USB-C, an AP2112K 3.3 V LDO, five 10 kΩ flex-sensor dividers on GPIO1–5, I2C and JTAG headers, and BOOT/reset buttons.
  • Sampling: 12-bit ADC at 50 Hz, each reading averaged over 8 samples.
  • Classifier: an MLP with 16 and 12 hidden units, trained from scratch in NumPy (Adam, Xavier init) and exported as a C header with the normalization constants. The output layer grows with the number of gestures: 326 parameters for 2 gestures, 365 for 5.
  • Custom classifier GUI: a Python app where I edit the gesture list, collect labeled data, train, deploy the weights to the firmware, and run the model live over USB or BLE. I can rename gestures to custom meanings (fist → resting) and export the session log as a CSV.
  • Output: 5-sample majority vote and a 0.6 confidence gate, then gesture and confidence on a 128×64 SSD1306 OLED.

Where I would use it

  • Wildlife behavior logs: map gestures to states such as resting, traveling, foraging, and log transitions while the binoculars stay up.
  • Transect tallies: classify what I just passed (healthy native species, invasive seedling) with one hand while the other holds a quadrat or walking stick.
  • Water-quality grading: read a test vial against a color chart and signal the grade without touching a phone with contaminated hands.

I haven't field-tested any of these yet. What I have measured is the classifier and the firmware, below.

Challenges

Eight rebuilds before it ran

The first boards did not work. I probed rails with a multimeter and scope, reworked joints, and rebuilt the board eight times. These are the faults I found:

What was wrongWhat I did
I had a bad chip on one of the buildsI swapped in a new part.
One LDO pin never touched groundI reflowed it so every LDO pin made contact.
Not enough solder reached the USB pins, so the board would not connect over USBI reworked the USB joints with better solder.
The AP2112K output read 3.7 V instead of 3.3 VI treated the regulator as damaged and replaced it.
USB still would not connectI rebuilt with good solder and new parts, then connected over UART. It ran, and I flashed the firmware from there.

Lessons

What I learned

  • I add UART as a backup now. When USB would not connect, flashing over UART is what got the board running.
  • I treat USB data lines carefully. Their impedance matters, and a joint with too little solder was enough to stop the board enumerating.
  • I probe before I rebuild. Measuring the rails would have found the LDO and ground faults sooner.

What I would change in the next revision

  • I would remove the capacitors on the USB lines.
  • I would remove the LED circuit on the enable pin. It formed a voltage divider.
  • I would remove the RC circuit on BOOT. BOOT should not have a time constant.
  • I would make the board much smaller. It worked, but it is larger than it needs to be.

Methods: how I measured each number

Data

3,267 labeled samples at 50 Hz for five gestures (fist, knife_hand, middle_finger, thumbs_up, triton), recorded by 3 people over 15 trials. Each row is label,ch0,…,ch4.

Accuracy

I replay the firmware pipeline in Python: min-max constants computed from the training folds only, inputs clipped to [0, 1] like the firmware, and a per-sample argmax. I split each recording into 5 contiguous chunks, train on four and test on the fifth, and drop 25 samples (0.5 s) around the held-out chunk from training so near-duplicate neighbors cannot leak. The result is the mean ± standard deviation over the 5 folds. The random 80/20 split gave 100%, which is why I use the blocked version.

Timing

A benchmark sketch runs on the board. Inference time is 5 batches of 1,000 forward passes timed with micros(). Cycle time is 200 passes at 50 Hz, timing the ADC read (5 channels × 8), normalize and forward pass, and the OLED update. It leaves out serial/BLE output and the vote.

Limits

  • The cross-validation is blocked in time, not split by person or by trial, so the training data contains every person. I have not measured accuracy on someone new or on a re-worn glove, so treat 97.8% as an upper bound. Leave-one-person-out is the next test.
  • The accuracy number does not include the vote or the confidence gate.
  • Timing is firmware-only, not finger-to-pixel latency.
  • Timing was measured on a 2-gesture model (326 parameters). The 5-gesture network has 365 and adds a few multiply-accumulates.
